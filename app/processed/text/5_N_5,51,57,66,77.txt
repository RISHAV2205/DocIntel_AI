 ABSTRACT: 
 Computational thinking (CT) is a problem-solving approach that involves breaking down 
complex tasks into manageable steps, recognizing patterns, abstracting key information, and 
developing effective algorithms to find solutions. It extends beyond programming, fostering 
logical thinking and creativity. However, rare is known how to effectively teach and learn 
computational thinking in early childhood education. To address this knowledge gap, the 
review conducted before examined 26 studies on the teaching and learning of computational 
thinking in early childhood education from 2010 to 2022. The content knowledge, tools, 
pedagogical design, assessment methods, and learning outcomes were analyze. Results 
indicated that, with age-appropriate instructional design, children could develop early 
concepts and skills of computational thinking, as well as other related skills such as 
communication, collaboration, and problem solving. By engaging young children in playful, 
hands-on activities, educators can nurture foundational CT skills, fostering critical thinking 
and preparing them for future learning in technology, science, and beyond. This paper 
explores the significance of CT, its core concept and its impact on cognitive development in 
early childhood.

INTRODUCTION: 
In recent years, the increasing integration of technology in education has led to a growing 
interest in introducing computational thinking (CT) at an early age. Computational thinking, 
which involves problem-solving techniques like decomposition, pattern recognition, 
abstraction, and algorithm design, has been widely recognized as an essential skill in the 
digital age. While CT has traditionally been associated with older students and adults, there 
is a rising interest in assessing its applicability and effectiveness in preschool education. This 
review explores the empirical study of computational concepts and their assessment in 
preschool students, focusing on their cognitive development, the effectiveness of various 
teaching approaches, and the impact on early learning. 
REVIEW OF LITERATURE 
The concept of Computational Thinking (CT) was first popularized by Jeanette Wing in 
2006, who emphasized its importance not only for computer scientists but for all 
individuals as a fundamental skill for problem-solving across disciplines. Since then, there 
has been increasing interest in integrating CT into education, with research focusing on the 
potential benefits of early exposure to computational concepts in preschool children. 
1. Definition of computation technology (CT): 
 
CT is a 21st century skill that influences our everyday life and learning . It is no longer 
merely considered as programming or computer skills that are required by computer 
scientists. Researchers have defined it as a positive digital mindsets, attitudes and 
readiness towards understanding and using this digital literacy skill in our everyday life 
. CT allows us to obtain thinking ways that are similar to that of a computer scientist 
when facing problems such as simplifying, embedding, transforming, simulation, and 
system design . In the stage of early childhood, children should not only develop their 
literacy skills such as reading, writing, and arithmetic, but also learn CT-related 
problem-solving skills such as logical thinking, sequencing abilities, abstraction, and 
algorithms. Regarding computational skills and computer science concepts, CT can be 
categorized by various problem-solving strategies such as sequencing, creative design, 
and content generation. 
 
However, CT is not restricted to using computers to learn. It is as a way of human 
thinking, a combination of mathematical and engineering thinking, problem-solving 
skills in our life that facilitate how people communicate and interact with others using 
CT tools. On top of cognitive skills and practices of computational and problem-solving 
skills, CT is further conceptualized as new perspectives in Brennan and Resnick 
framework. In the model, students could gain perspectives that they interpret about the 
world around them and about themselves in terms of expressing, connecting and 
questioning.

2. CT in early childhood education: 
Recent empirical studies have delved deeper into how computational thinking can be 
effectively introduced in preschool settings. Sullivan and Bers (2016) conducted one 
of the early studies assessing the impact of robotics on young children’s computational 
thinking skills. Their research showed that children aged 4 to 6 were capable of using 
simple robots to understand basic programming concepts such as sequencing, cause-
and-effect relationships, and debugging. This study underscored the idea that 
computational thinking can be taught through concrete, physical activities that engage 
children's natural curiosity. And also introducing CT concepts at a young age can foster 
creativity, enhance critical thinking, and develop early problem-solving abilities which 
help him/her to solve real life problem when they grew up 
Similarly, Papadakis et al. (2021) highlighted the effectiveness of integrating digital 
tools, such as apps and games, to teach computational concepts to young learners. Their 
research demonstrated that preschoolers not only enjoy engaging with digital tools but 
also exhibit improved cognitive and problem-solving skills when exposed to 
computational tasks in a structured, playful environment. 
 
3. Assessment of computation thinking in young children: 
Assessing CT in young children presents unique challenges, particularly because 
traditional assessment methods are not well-suited to early learners. Horn et al. (2012) 
and Brennan and Resnick (2012) provided frameworks for assessing computational 
thinking skills in children using non-traditional methods such as task-based evaluations, 
observations, and digital interactions. These assessments focus on how children 
approach tasks that involve pattern recognition, sequencing, and abstraction, rather than 
relying on written tests or quizzes. 
In their study, Duncan et al. (2017) examined various strategies to assess CT in 
preschoolers through observations of children's play. They found that children who 
engaged in CT-related activities, such as sorting and sequencing, demonstrated early 
understanding of problem-solving processes akin to those used in more formal 
computational thinking. 
Types of assessment used: 
Several assessment methods have been developed to evaluate the computational skills 
of preschool children. These include: 
• 
Observation-Based Assessment: Teachers observe how children engage in 
activities that require sequencing, pattern recognition, or problem-solving. For 
instance, educators may provide puzzles or games that encourage logical thinking 
and observe how children approach these tasks. 
• 
Task-Based Assessment: Structured tasks designed to measure specific 
computational skills, such as sorting objects based on characteristics or following 
step-by-step instructions to complete an activity, are used to gauge understanding. 
• 
Interactive Digital Tools: In some studies, digital tools and applications that focus 
on basic CT concepts are used to engage children in problem-solving activities. 
Children's interactions with these tools are monitored and evaluated to assess their 
understanding of the concepts 
      Factor influencing assessment outcome: 
Several factors influence how well preschoolers grasp computational concepts. These   
include their cognitive development, prior exposure to technology, and the methods 
used to introduce CT. Social factors, such as collaboration with peers and guidance 
from educators, also play a significant role in helping children understand and apply 
computational thinking 
    
4. Impact of CT in cognitive development: 
The link between computational thinking and cognitive development has been a major 
focus of research in early childhood education. Resnick and Siegel (2015) noted that 
computational thinking activities enhance children's problem-solving abilities, as they 
encourage logical reasoning and creativity. Their work on the Scratch Jr. platform 
demonstrated that children who participated in creative coding activities developed 
better reasoning and communication skills, as well as enhanced ability to solve 
complex tasks through collaboration. 
Further supporting this view, Lye and Koh (2014) emphasized the role of CT in 
developing cognitive flexibility and executive functioning. They argued that children 
who are exposed to CT early on not only develop technical skills but also improve their 
overall cognitive abilities, including memory, attention, and perseverance. 
 
5. Games and digital tool in CT education: 
Fessakis et al. (2013) examined the role of digital games in fostering computational 
thinking in young children. Their study focused on the use of educational games that 
teach children to solve puzzles, recognize patterns, and follow algorithms. The findings 
revealed that game-based learning is particularly effective in engaging young children 
and enhancing their problem-solving abilities. The visual and interactive nature of these 
games allows preschoolers to grasp complex concepts in a fun and accessible way. 
Moreover, Lye and Koh (2018) highlighted that digital tools such as ScratchJr and 
other programming apps provide a structured yet flexible environment for young 
learners. These tools have been shown to improve children's ability to break down tasks 
and engage in step-by-step problem-solving, essential for computational thinking 
 
6. Gender and diversity consideration: 
Empirical studies have shown no significant gender differences in the ability to grasp 
computational concepts at the preschool level, indicating that both boys and girls can 
benefit equally from early exposure to CT. However, diversity in socioeconomic 
background plays a role in access to resources and prior exposure to technology, which 
can affect the outcomes of computational concept assessments. 
 
7. Effectiveness in teaching method: 
The effectiveness of teaching methods is a key focus of many empirical studies. Play-
based learning has been identified as one of the most successful strategies for 
introducing CT concepts to preschoolers. Interactive games, both physical and digital, 
engage children while promoting problem-solving skills in a fun and low-pressure 
environment. Additionally, hands-on activities such as building blocks, puzzles, and 
board games can introduce core CT concepts such as sequencing and abstraction 
without the need for screens or formal programming. 
 
8. Challenges in teaching CT in preschool: 
While there are numerous benefits to introducing CT in early education, there are also 
challenges, particularly related to the developmental stage of preschoolers. Young 
children have limited abstract thinking abilities and short attention spans, which can 
make it difficult to introduce certain computational concepts directly. Furthermore, the 
availability of age-appropriate tools and resources for teaching CT to young learners is 
still in the developmental stages.

9. Role of technology in enhancing CT education: 
Digital tools and resources have been instrumental in introducing computational 
thinking to young children. Resnick et al. (2013) explored the use of platforms like 
ScratchJr, a simplified version of Scratch, which allows preschoolers to create simple 
programs through drag-and-drop blocks. These tools not only make computational 
concepts more accessible to young learners but also foster creativity and problem-
solving in a highly interactive environment. 
Tu et al. (2020) highlighted that tablets, robots, and other educational technologies can 
enhance children’s engagement with CT concepts, providing immediate feedback and 
interactive learning opportunities. However, their research also pointed out the 
importance of integrating screen-based activities with hands-on play to ensure balanced 
cognitive development. 
10. Cultural and socioeconomic factor: 
Cultural and socioeconomic factors also influence how preschool children engage with 
computational thinking. DiSessa (2018) explored the "digital divide," noting that 
children from lower-income families often have less access to technology and 
educational resources that facilitate CT learning. Sullivan et al. (2017) argued for more 
equitable access to CT education through affordable tools and community-based 
programs to ensure all children can benefit from early exposure to computational 
thinking. 
 
10. Finding: 
Although CT is an essential topic and has been examined around the globe, it has been 
inadequately investigated in the ECE context – 26 studies were identified in the 
literature from 2011 to 2022. We assumed that this number of research articles is 
sufficient to provide an exploratory view of early CT education

Conclusions : 
Introducing computational thinking (CT) in preschool holds significant potential for 
improving children's problem-solving, creativity, and logical thinking. The future of CT 
education looks bright, with prospects for cross-curricular integration and enhanced 
assessment methods. However, challenges like balancing screen time with hands-on 
activities and ensuring equitable access to technology must be carefully managed. In 
my opinion, CT offers essential skills for the digital age and, when introduced 
thoughtfully, can foster both cognitive and social development. By providing inclusive 
and engaging opportunities, we can better prepare young learners for future academic 
and real-world success.
